% I. WHAT THIS NETWORK IS FOR
% =====================================================================
\section{What This Network Is For}\label{sec:thesis}

\newthought{Axona is a communication network with no owner} --- no company,
no server, no place in the middle where anyone sits who could read, rank,
charge, throttle, or forbid. Not because an operator promises restraint, but
because by construction there is nowhere for one to be. It runs in
production on ordinary phones, browsers, and laptops.

The keystone is one design choice: \textbf{authorship is a signature, not an
account.} Who is speaking is a key the speaker holds. Where they are and how
they connect is a separate fact the network never links to it. The network
moves signed bytes between endpoints and does nothing else --- so it
\emph{cannot act on} what you say. It cannot rank it, suppress it, or reveal
who you are, because it was never told and never needed to know.

That is the whole property. Everything below is machinery for keeping it
true under churn, partition, and load.

\marginnote{This section is deliberately short and deliberately first. An
implementer who knows only the frames will make locally reasonable choices
that violate the thesis --- adding a return address to make debugging
easier, caching an identity to save a round trip. Those choices are the
failure mode this section exists to prevent.}

\subsection{What the thesis has already decided}

These follow from the property above, not from taste. An implementation that
breaks one is not a variant of Axona; it is a different network wearing the
same wire format. Each has been re-litigated at least once and settled.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{The signed envelope discloses \emph{who}, never \emph{where}.}
  No node id, no region, no address in the envelope --- ever. This was
  proposed twice for good-looking reasons and reverted twice. The routing
  wrapper is a different object with different rules
  (\S\ref{sec:notclaimed}).
\item \textbf{Transport identity does not persist.} A stable node id across
  restarts is a correlator, and a correlator plus an IP is a physical
  location. Restart must yield a different node id. Author identity is the
  opposite: it persists, because provenance is the point.
\item \textbf{Region is an optimization, never a wall.} The geographic byte
  places traffic; it never gates admission or capability. A sparse region
  must roll over to its neighbours rather than refuse service. A node that
  cannot be served locally is still served.
\item \textbf{No content-based anything in the kernel.} No ranking, no
  content filtering, no priority by payload. The kernel does not model what
  a message says and must not acquire the ability. Moderation is a
  subscriber-side choice over signed objects, never a network-side one.
\item \textbf{Retraction is the author's, not the network's.} A creator can
  kill their own message and the network will carry the tombstone. Nobody
  else can, and there is no operator who could be compelled to.
\item \textbf{No accounts, no directory, no operator.} Bootstrap uses
  bridges, and a bridge is a convenience that the protocol must survive
  losing. If any mechanism here only works while a bridge is up, that is a
  defect, not a design.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What it costs}

A network that cannot be made to take sides cannot be made to take the right
side either. The same property that lets a researcher under sanctions
collaborate as an equal lets a disinformation campaign route around every
attempt to slow it. It gives AI agents from different vendors somewhere to
meet as peers, and it gives a misaligned agent the same place on the same
terms.

This document does not resolve that, and no clever framing should be
expected to. The judgement is that a tool no one can control is worth
building for the same reason humanity kept fire and built hearths and fire
brigades rather than giving it back --- and that the answer to such a tool is
not a central authority who controls it, since that only rebuilds the
intermediary the design set out to remove.

Where this note is silent and you must choose, choose the option that keeps
the network unable to act on content, unable to link authorship to location,
and able to keep running when any particular participant vanishes. That
ordering has settled every genuine ambiguity so far.

\section{How to Read This Document}

This note is two documents sharing one text. Read as prose, it explains
what Axona is and why each mechanism exists --- every rule in this system
was paid for by a specific failure, and the failures are named. Read as a
specification, it contains the architectural model and the current wire
contract: identifier derivations
(\S\ref{sec:identity}), the complete wire vocabulary with payload schemas
(\S\ref{sec:wire}, \S\ref{sec:pubsub}), the root-management decision table
(\S\ref{sec:roots}), the timing model (\S\ref{sec:timing}), and the
invariants any implementation must uphold (\S\ref{sec:invariants}).
Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction} is addressed directly to an implementer
--- human or AI --- and orders the work. It will not yet carry you to a
peer that interoperates byte-for-byte with the deployed network;
\S\ref{sec:notclaimed} says exactly where it stops.

Three conventions. \emph{Normative} material (schemas, constants,
invariants, algorithms) appears in tables and numbered rules; prose around
it is \emph{narrative} and explains intent. Feynman's epigraph is the
acceptance test: a reader who cannot re-derive the mechanism from this
note has found a bug in the note, and should report it as such. And
every constant is given with its value \emph{and} its reason --- a
constant whose reason you don't know is one you will mis-tune.

\marginnote{Companion notes: \emph{Root Management (RootClaim state
machine)} for the full election deep-dive; the \emph{Kernel Refactor

\section{The System in One Page}

Axona is a peer-to-peer message substrate: publish/subscribe over a
distributed hash table, with no servers in the data path. Every
participant --- a browser tab, a phone, a headless Node process --- is a
\emph{peer} holding a keypair. Peers connect to each other directly over
WebRTC data channels (browsers) or WebSockets (servers), discover each
other through the DHT, and exchange \emph{routed messages}: each message
is addressed to a 264-bit identifier and forwarded, hop by hop, to the
live peer whose own identifier is closest to the target by XOR distance.

Everything else is built from that one primitive:

\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{topic} is a 264-bit id derived from its name, owner, and
  write policy. Publishing routes the message toward the topic id; the
  peer where routing terminates is the topic's \textbf{root} --- emergent,
  never elected. The root timestamps (\emph{stamps}) each message, giving
  the topic a total order, caches recent history, and fans deliveries out
  to subscribers.
\item A \textbf{subscription} is a periodically renewed routed message
  toward the same topic id. The renewal is simultaneously the keepalive,
  the failure detector, the gap-recovery request, and the self-heal: if
  anything about the path or the root has changed, the renewal finds the
  new truth and re-attaches.
\item \textbf{Durability} is replication among the peers XOR-closest to
  the topic (the \emph{cohort}), plus explicit hand-off when a root
  departs gracefully.
\item \textbf{Identity} is cryptographic and self-authenticating: a
  message is believed because it verifies against the author key it
  carries, never because of who relayed it. There is no certificate
  authority, no account database, and no server that can revoke a
  publisher.
\end{itemize}

A network has any number of bridge servers, each for \emph{signaling
only}: a bridge introduces new peers so they can open direct
connections. Bridges federate by
uplinking to each other as ordinary peers, advertise themselves on a
public directory topic, and clients rank the directory and fail over
(\S\ref{sec:transport}). A bridge is bootstrap infrastructure, not a
data-path relay; two peers with established mesh connections continue
exchanging messages if every bridge is down,\marginnote{Proven live
2026-07-12: a fresh authenticated data channel formed between two peers
with the bridge process dead, signaling relayed over the mesh (19/19,
bulk 100\%).} and new connections can even be signaled across the
existing mesh.

The design bets on one asymmetry, everywhere: \emph{wrong claims that
self-correct are cheaper than coordination that prevents them}. Roots
are claimed optimistically and reconciled by evidence; publishes are
retried idempotently rather than acknowledged; caches converge by
anti-entropy rather than consensus. The protocol has no leader, no
quorum, and no distributed lock anywhere.

The bet has a boundary, and the boundary was paid for in
production:\marginnote{The 4.24.0 release let a departing node plant
replica state on its cohort as a durability fallback --- locally
reasonable, and it collapsed the testnet backbone twice in one night.
The post-mortem is Kernel-Refactor-Analysis v0.2 \S1.} the optimism
applies to \emph{claims} --- statements that evidence can cheaply
revoke --- never to \emph{standing state}. A wrong claim costs its
holder one demotion; wrong standing state costs whoever must maintain
or evict it, forever if no one does. So claims are optimistic, but
state obeys two laws stated as invariants below: it is bounded by
demand, never by churn history (I-10), and it may be planted on
another node only by a principal alive to maintain it (the
principal-liveness rule, \S\ref{sec:pubsub}).

% IV. IDENTITY AND ADDRESSING
% =====================================================================
\section{Identity and Addressing}\label{sec:identity}

Everything in Axona lives in one keyspace: \textbf{264-bit identifiers},
written on the wire as 66 lowercase hex characters, handled internally as
\texttt{BigInt}. An id is always \texttt{regionByte}\,$\Vert$\,\texttt{256-bit body}. The high byte is an S2 geographic cell (a coarse region code);
the remaining 256 bits are cryptographic material. Distance between ids
is XOR, compared as unsigned integers; the region byte's position makes
same-region ids numerically close, so proximity in the keyspace loosely
tracks proximity on the planet.

The S2 cube yields 192 cells, but roughly half are open ocean or near-empty
land where no peers live. Minting an id there would be a mistake: a topic
anchored in empty water has no local population, so the nodes nearest its
region byte --- a boundary sliver of the closest real region --- would absorb
\emph{every} topic anchored anywhere in that ocean, a hotspot.\marginnote{Sim
A/B (2{,}100 nodes, 2{,}000 ocean-anchored topics): the fold cuts peak
per-node load $2.7\times$ and busiest-1\% share $1.9\times$.} So every id is
minted through \texttt{canonicalRegion()}, which \emph{folds} each ocean or
sparse cell onto its nearest \textbf{major} (populated) region --- 84 of them.
A node in mid-Pacific and a topic anchored there both resolve to Hawaii's
region and spread across its full node population. The fold is deterministic,
carried in the id itself (no coordinator), and wire-compatible: every populated
region keeps its exact byte. Regions are labelled with neutral in-range
\emph{animal} names (\texttt{eagle}, \texttt{chinkara}, \texttt{penguin})
rather than country names --- a country name on a border-spanning cell is
needlessly political, and the label is presentation only (the id carries the
numeric code, never the name).

\subsection{Node identity (where routing can reach you)}

\texttt{createNodeIdentity(\{lat, lng\})} generates an Ed25519 keypair
and derives \texttt{nodeId = regionByte(lat,lng)}\,$\Vert$\,\texttt{SHA-256(pubkey)}.
The node key authenticates \emph{transport}: channel establishment,
handshakes, routing --- never content. Node identity is typically
ephemeral; a peer that restarts with a fresh key is simply a new node.

\subsection{Author identity (who said it)}

\texttt{createAuthorIdentity()} generates a separate, location-free
Ed25519 keypair; the \texttt{authorId} is the public key. Author keys
sign \emph{content} and are usually persistent (the browser app stores
them in IndexedDB, non-extractable). The separation is deliberate and
load-bearing: the network learns \emph{where} a node is from its node id
but only \emph{who} authored a message from its envelope --- a signed
envelope discloses the author, never the author's location, and no
protocol change may add a node-id or region to the envelope.\marginnote{
This rule has been defended twice against convenience patches. Location
display, where an application wants it, is app-level and opt-in.}

\subsection{Topics (structured addresses with policy folded in)}

A topic is addressed by a \emph{descriptor}
$\{$\texttt{region}, \texttt{owner}, \texttt{name}, \texttt{write}$\}$:
\texttt{region} a region name or code; \texttt{owner} an authorId or
null; \texttt{name} a UTF-8 string; \texttt{write} either
\texttt{'open'} (anyone publishes) or \texttt{'owner'} (only the owner's
key). The id is:

\begin{lstlisting}
topicId = regionByte
          || SHA-256( canonical({ owner, name, write }) )
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent with the region byte resolved by rule: an explicit
\texttt{region} wins; otherwise the \emph{publisher's own node region}
is used; otherwise the derivation throws
(\texttt{TOPIC\_REGION\_REQUIRED}). The region is \textbf{never} derived
from the author key --- the author has no location, and hashing the
authorId into a region would dump every one of an author's topics into
one arbitrary cell, a hotspot. (An earlier design did exactly that and
was removed in 3.1.0.) Whichever rule fires, the resulting byte passes
through \texttt{canonicalRegion()}, so a topic can never be anchored at
an unpopulated cell.

\texttt{write} defaults on whether an owner is named, and the default
is the safe one: no owner $\rightarrow$ the topic is necessarily
\texttt{'open'} (any passed \texttt{write} is ignored); owner named
$\rightarrow$ \texttt{'owner'} unless \texttt{'open'} is passed
explicitly (an owner-namespaced inbox anyone may post to). Forgetting
\texttt{write} on an owned feed must not leave it world-writable
(3.2.0). Because the write policy is folded into the hash, a topic's
policy is not mutable state --- a root recomputes the id from the signed
descriptor and enforces \texttt{write:'owner'} statelessly.

\subsection{Messages (content-addressed, author-signed)}

Every published message travels as an \textbf{envelope}:

\begin{lstlisting}
{ msgId:  hex64,            // content address, see below
  seq:    int,              // per-publisher monotonic
  ts:     ms-epoch,         // publisher clock
  topic:  { region, owner, name, write },   // the signed descriptor
  message: <any JSON value>,
  signature?:   'ed25519:' + hex128,
  signerPubkey?: hex64 }    // the author key (absent = anonymous)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{msgId = SHA-256(canonical(\{publisher, message\}))} where
  \texttt{publisher} is \texttt{signerPubkey} or null --- a stable
  content address of (author, message), independent of time, topic, or
  signature. Identical re-publishes collapse wherever both copies meet
  retained state for the \emph{same topic} --- dedup is topic-local and
  retention-bounded, not network-wide, and the same body under two topics
  is two messages. A publisher wanting distinct ids adds a nonce to
  \texttt{message}.
\item The signature covers the domain-tagged core
  \texttt{canonical(\{d,} \texttt{seq,} \texttt{ts,} \texttt{topic,}
  \texttt{message\})} with \texttt{d} the envelope domain tag ---
  domain separation means an envelope signature can never be replayed as
  a kill signature or a channel-binding value; signing the resolved
  descriptor binds the message to the exact topic \emph{and policy}.
\item \texttt{canonical()} is a total, JSON-valid canonical encoding
  with recursively sorted keys, shared by every signing and hashing site
  in the system. It is \emph{not} RFC~8785: it inherits JavaScript
  \texttt{JSON.stringify} value semantics, including dropped
  \texttt{undefined} object members and coerced non-finite numbers. An
  independent implementation must reproduce those semantics, not JCS.
  Golden vectors are not yet published (\S\ref{sec:notclaimed}).
\item Freshness: receivers enforce a bound on \texttt{ts} skew at live
  ingress, then deduplicate by \texttt{msgId} against that topic's
  retained cache and tombstones. There is \emph{no} per-author sequence
  high-water mark --- the anti-replay property is the signed timestamp
  window plus topic-local id dedup, and it is bounded by retention.
  Replayed history is exempt from the ts bound but capped by a
  future-tolerance rule (\S\ref{sec:pubsub}).
\end{itemize}

A \textbf{kill} (retraction) is its own signed object naming
\texttt{(topicId, msgId)}, domain-tagged, and valid only when signed by
\emph{the same author key} that signed the target message --- creator-only
retraction, enforced at every root, with a provisional path when the kill
races ahead of its target (\S\ref{sec:pubsub}).

\section{Routing}\label{sec:routing}

Each peer maintains a \textbf{synaptome}: a bounded table (target
$\sim$50 entries) of live, authenticated, directly-connected neighbours,
mixing keyspace-near peers (completeness around one's own id) with
long-range contacts (reach). Entries are first-party only --- gossip
feeds a \emph{candidate pool}, and a candidate becomes a synapse only
after a verified direct connection, which is the eclipse defense: no
one can write into another peer's routing table.

Two search primitives share the table:

\begin{description}
\item[Greedy routing (\texttt{routeMessage})] forward to the neighbour
  strictly closest to the target; the peer where no neighbour improves
  on self is the \emph{terminal}. One-pass, no backtracking, hop-budgeted.
  Fast and usually right; on a sparse or divergent mesh it can strand in
  a local minimum --- every convergence mechanism in
  \S\ref{sec:roots} exists to correct exactly this.
\item[Iterative lookup (\texttt{findKClosest} / \texttt{lookup})] the
  classic $\alpha$-parallel iterative search, querying successively
  closer peers for their neighbours. Slower (multiple round trips) but
  global: it escapes local minima and crosses regions. The kernel
  \emph{never} blocks a send on it --- lookups warm hints in the
  background (\S\ref{sec:roots}).
\end{description}

\noindent Two standing rules. \textbf{The bridge is never a data hop}:
routing skips the bridge id even when it is numerically closest ---
signaling infrastructure must not become a root, a relay, or a
convergence anchor. \textbf{Region is a placement hint, not a wall}:
the region byte clusters same-region ids, but a lookup crosses regions
freely; treating the prefix as a routing wall caused a cross-region
split-brain (fixed 4.17.1) and is now an explicit anti-pattern.

Departed peers are marked dead immediately on channel loss (eviction,
graceful-leave notify) and skipped by routing; reachability to a
\emph{newcomer} lives in its neighbours' tables and is healed by inbound
traffic --- which is why cold publishers re-send by design
(\S\ref{sec:timing}) rather than waiting to be found.

One property of greedy routing surprises every instrument pointed at
it: \textbf{a routed message need not touch the wire at all}. The
origin resolves its next hop and, finding none closer than itself,
delivers locally at hop zero --- so when a publisher happens to be the
topic-closest node, its publish is consumed in-process and no PUB frame
exists anywhere. This is correct, it is how a solo node roots its own
topics, and it happens to roughly one publish in $N$ on an $N$-node
network, at random, by topology. A test that asserts ``a publish
produces traffic'' is therefore wrong one time in $N$; a packet capture
at the publisher shows the \emph{replication} frames that follow and
none of the publish itself. Measure delivery at a subscriber, never
presence on a wire.

\section{Transports and the Bridge}\label{sec:transport}

The kernel is transport-agnostic behind one interface; three
implementations ship. \texttt{Transport.web()} --- WebRTC data channels
between peers, WebSocket to the bridge for signaling; the browser
production path. \texttt{Transport.node()} --- WebSocket server-to-server;
relays and bridges. \texttt{Transport.sim()} --- in-process, for the
simulator and tests.

\subsection{Authentication: \texttt{axona/5}}

Every channel is mutually authenticated before any Axona frame flows.
The handshake proves possession of the node key via a signed,
domain-tagged challenge bound to the channel: for WebRTC, the
channel-binding value folds both sides' \emph{DTLS certificate
fingerprints} (a MITM terminating DTLS changes the fingerprints and the
signature fails); for WebSockets, the binding uses the connection's
nonces. A peer's claimed nodeId must carry \texttt{SHA-256(pubkey)} as its
low 256 bits for the proven key. The \emph{region byte is deliberately
not covered}: a node picks its own area code, and admission neither
verifies nor cares where it claims to be. Identity is verified;
\emph{location is asserted}, and the difference matters to any threat
model that reasons about regional concentration.

\subsection{Version discipline}

Two version axes ride the hello frames. \texttt{WIRE\_VERSION} (currently
\texttt{4.0}) partitions hermetically on its major --- wire-3.x and
wire-4.x reject each other at both the bridge gate and the peer-peer
handshake, because 4.x changed convergence and replay semantics.
\texttt{KERNEL\_VERSION} (currently \texttt{4.59.2}) is informative and
surfaced at every runtime surface (healthz, welcome, UI) --- an
observability rule, not a gate; wire-compatible kernels interoperate
freely, and deploys are staggered on exactly that property.

\subsection{The bridge, and life without it}

The bridge accepts WebSocket connections, runs the version gate and the
authenticated hello, mints short-lived TURN credentials, and
\emph{introduces} peers: a joining peer receives a set of live peers to
open WebRTC connections to, and the bridge ferries the SDP/ICE signaling
for those first connections. The bridge embeds an ordinary kernel peer
(it hosts keyspace, participates in routing for signaling purposes) but
is excluded from data-path roles by every selection rule.

Join flow: connect WSS $\rightarrow$ version gate $\rightarrow$
authenticated hello $\rightarrow$ welcome (peer list, TURN) $\rightarrow$
open direct channels $\rightarrow$ synaptome admission per connection
$\rightarrow$ the peer is routable. \texttt{connect()} wraps the whole
sequence in one call. After bootstrap the bridge is optional:
\emph{mesh-relayed signaling} routes SDP/ICE across existing data
channels, so new direct connections form bridge-free, and existing
connections never depended on it.

The bridge can also say so out loud. A meshed client is a slot the
bridge no longer needs, so the bridge closes it with a
\emph{graduation} code (4200). A client holding at least the
graduation floor of authenticated mesh peers keeps its mesh and stops
reconnecting; a watchdog re-dials the bridge only if the mesh later
thins. A client that was not actually meshed reconnects immediately ---
which makes the whole scheme self-correcting, so the bridge can
graduate optimistically and let the client's own state decide. Before
4.35.0 the client read the graduation close as a failure and tore its
mesh down to zero peers on the way out --- the bridge politely
dismissing a student and the student burning down the school.

Bridges self-advertise on the public topic
\texttt{axona:bridge-directory} (each bridge \texttt{host()}s and
republishes it); clients collect the directory at launch, rank, and fail
over. Multiple bridges federate by uplinking to each other as ordinary
peers, so their peer populations form one mesh.

\subsection{Judging clients fairly}

A bridge judges client liveness only over time it was actually
listening: an event-loop stall on the bridge re-arms client grace and
suspends idle sweeps until the taint ages out (invariant I-5). A client
must never be dropped for the server's own pause.

\section{The Wire Protocol}\label{sec:wire}

All frames are JSON. Above the transport layer there are three frame
families: the handshake/hello family (\S\ref{sec:transport}), direct
notifications (\texttt{direct\_*}, point-to-point over an existing
channel), and \emph{routed messages} --- the workhorse. A routed message
carries \texttt{\{type, payload, targetId, hops, originId\}} and is
forwarded greedily toward \texttt{targetId}. The handler registered for
\texttt{type} runs \emph{at every hop}, not only at the terminal: each
receiver dispatches locally --- with \texttt{meta.isTerminal} telling it
which it is --- and only then decides whether to forward. Waypoint
behaviour is a consequence of that, not a special case.\marginnote{\small
\texttt{originId} travels with every routed frame. The signed envelope
carries no node id, but the wrapper moving it does; see
\S\ref{sec:notclaimed}.}

Pub/sub message types. This list is \textbf{complete and normative} ---
all seventeen routed types the v4.59.2 kernel emits or accepts. The
per-frame \emph{payload schemas} are not: they are described in prose in
\S\ref{sec:pubsub} and are not yet published as machine-readable
schemas with required/optional fields, caps, and receiver-validation
verdicts. An implementer can name every frame from this table and still
be unable to construct several of them. That gap is
\S\ref{sec:notclaimed}'s first item and closes with the conformance
vectors.\marginnote{\small A missing type is worse than an undocumented
one: it makes an implementation look conformant while silently dropping a
verb. \texttt{pubsub:pullresp} was absent from this table until the
2026-08-04 audit read it out of \texttt{constants.js}.}

\begin{table*}[h]
\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Type & Routed toward & Meaning \\
\midrule
\texttt{pubsub:sub} & topic (or via) & subscribe / renew; carries since, hw, lw \\
\texttt{pubsub:unsub} & topic (or via) & explicit unsubscribe \\
\texttt{pubsub:pub} & topic (or via) & publish; unstamped envelope \\
\texttt{pubsub:deliver} & subscriber & stamped messages (and del markers) \\
\texttt{pubsub:adopt} & child & delegate: become child relay, take batch \\
\texttt{pubsub:pullup} & holder & ``you are ahead of me --- replay up'' \\
\texttt{pubsub:replayup} & parent & stamped cache delta, upward \\
\texttt{pubsub:handoff} & heir & departing root pushes history \\
\texttt{pubsub:handoffack} & leaver & heir confirms receipt (quenches retry/fallback) \\

\texttt{pubsub:rootbeacon} & neighbours & soft-state root advertisement \\

\textbf{Via waypoints.} Topic-addressed payloads may carry
\texttt{via:\,[hex,\ldots]} (capped at 8), an ordered waypoint list: the
message routes toward \texttt{via[0]} first. At the terminal for a
waypoint that is not the local node, the waypoint is \emph{popped} and
the message re-sent toward the next (finally the bare topic id). This is
the oldest self-healing rule in the system --- \emph{a dead waypoint
always falls through to the topic id and re-seats} --- and it is
load-bearing: pinned subscriptions to departed relays heal by exactly
this mechanism, and any analysis that forgets it will wrongly predict
deadlocks.\marginnote{An external review made exactly this error in July
2026; the trace is in the Root-Management review response.}

\textbf{The topic decision.} Every topic-addressed handler first
classifies: \emph{handle} (I am \texttt{via[0]} and hold the role, or I
am the bare-topic terminal and region-permitted), \emph{reroute} (dead
or foreign waypoint at my terminal --- pop and continue),
\emph{forward} (not terminal), or \emph{reject} (terminal but the
region lock forbids rooting here; enabled only when
\texttt{configureRegionLock(\{enforce:true\})}, which is OFF
pre-critical-mass).

% VIII. PUB/SUB: THE AXON TREE
% =====================================================================
\section{Pub/Sub: The Axon Tree}\label{sec:pubsub}

Pub/sub is \emph{routing-only}: every interaction is a routed message;
there are no direct pub/sub connections, no root sets, no K-closest
fan-out at publish time. One rule generates the whole design: \emph{the
peer where a topic-addressed message terminates acts for the topic}.

\subsection{Roles, natures, and per-topic state}

A peer holding any responsibility for a topic keeps a \texttt{role}:
\texttt{\{topicId, isRoot, subscribers (map subHex $\to$ \{since,
lastRenewed\}), children (set of child-relay ids), cache (ascending by
stamp), cacheIds, tombstones, seq, lastTs, replicas, replSig,
replLastFull, backupOf, lastReplicaAt, pulledLw, probeTries, probeAt,
metricsOn\}}. \texttt{isRoot} is the root-claim state machine's state
and changes \emph{only} through it (\S\ref{sec:roots}); the remaining
fields are the convergence plane's per-topic memory
(\S\ref{sec:pubsub}, ``Convergence'') --- each is named where its
mechanism is described.

A role acts in exactly one of four \textbf{natures}. Each nature
carries \emph{obligations} (work the node performs while in it) and a
named \emph{eviction path} (how the role ends). This table is
normative, and its discipline is invariant I-10: standing state
without an evictor is a leak; obligations without a live principal are
a storm.

\begin{table*}[h]
\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
Nature & Marked by & Obligations & Evicted by \\
\midrule
ROOT & \texttt{isRoot} & stamp, beacon, verify, replicate, serve & demotion / idle sweep \\
CHILD & in parent's \texttt{children} & renew upstream, re-fan down once & subscriber loss + idle sweep \\
BACKUP & \texttt{backupOf} set & hold warm copy; subscribe as election standby & principal gone + re-homed + 60\,s \\
HOLDER & hosted / app-subscribed & renew; advertise \texttt{hw} & unhost / unsub / TTL \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table*}

The natures compose (a HOLDER may be ROOT; a BACKUP is also a CHILD
while its principal lives), but every write of a role onto a node must
identify which nature it creates and therefore which evictor applies.
The first production collapse this system suffered was a nature nobody
had modeled --- a BACKUP whose principal was already gone, created by a
departing node, whose subscribe obligation ran forever with no eviction
path in sight (\S\ref{sec:invariants}, I-10).

\subsection{Admission: a node can say no}

The neuromorphic layer has had capacity discipline from the start: a
hard synaptome budget (50), a refusal path, and breadth-then-depth
spreading under the cap. The axonic layer had none of it --- no budget,
no refusal, no spreading. David's pointer was exact: ``the system does
a pretty good job of spreading connections at the neuromorphic layer;
we need to do something similar on the axonic side.'' The bill for the
gap arrived on 2026-07-26: nine production relays on three 961\,MB
droplets, per-relay role counts of 325, 431, 523 and climbing past
720, memory at 86--92\%, five of the nine locked out of the bridge ---
the join-storm spiral, fed by nodes that could not decline the roles
drowning them.

Since 4.46.0 every role acquisition passes \texttt{canAcceptRole()}:
one gate, four reasons, two tiers. \textbf{Bridge} is the hard tier and
the floor may never override it --- a bridge is transport and
introduction, nothing else; \texttt{host()} was removed from bridges
and \texttt{sub()} kept rooting anyway, so a soft tier here would
reopen that door on a timer. The soft tier: \textbf{not-seated} (a
node under 90\,s old \emph{or} without a routable non-bridge neighbour
--- not a bare timer, because the locked-out relays sat at 0 open
channels and 58 bound, and a timer would hand roles to exactly those
nodes the moment it expired), \textbf{saturated}, and \textbf{paced}
(more than 4 new roles this tick). Every reason is a property of the
node's \emph{own} state, self-declared and self-limiting: refusal can
only make a node hold less, never acquire what it is not closest to.
It is not an exception to the address rule.

\textbf{The floor is mandatory.} If every candidate refuses, nobody
roots --- and both soft reasons can do that fleet-wide. A simultaneous
restart puts everyone in grace; a loaded backbone makes everyone
saturated; both happened on production the same day. So a soft refusal
with no alternative candidate is admitted anyway and logged
\texttt{admitted-despite}. A grace period that can partition the
network is worse than no grace period.

\textbf{From a count to a measurement.} The original ceiling was
\texttt{MAX\_ROLES} $=$ 96, and production disproved it as an
instrument: relays ran 184 to 1{,}182 roles --- seven to twelve times
the ceiling --- because the mandatory floor must admit every terminal
role or drop the message. The count carries no information: 96 idle
roles is nothing, 96 hot ones may be fatal, and the same count means
different things on different hardware. David's question --- ``can a
node measure its ability to manage the roles it has?'' --- replaced
the count with pressure measured against real protocol deadlines, at
the points where obligations actually complete (4.47--4.53). The
metric's own first version could not report failure: it stamped every
role serviced at the \emph{top} of the tick, before any work, and read
zero while a role sat 95\,s past its 60\,s replication deadline. The
stamp moved to completion.

\textbf{CAVEAT: this surface is not finished.} Refusal exists and is
fenced; pressure is measured where obligations complete; what is
\emph{partial} is the feedback from measured pressure back into
admission and shedding. The health scorecard tracks the gap; a reader
should treat ``a node can say no'' as shipped and ``a node sheds load
before failing'' as in progress.

\subsection{Subscribe: attach, renew, re-home}

\texttt{sub(topic, \{since\})} sends
\texttt{pubsub:sub \{topicId, subscriberId, since, hw, via, latest?\}}.
\texttt{since} is the replay floor: a timestamp, \texttt{0}
(``\texttt{since:'all'}'' --- full retained history), or now (live
tail); \texttt{latest} additionally folds the newest retained entry into
the first delivery regardless of age. \texttt{hw} advertises the
sender's own cache high-water --- the durability hook: a root that is
\emph{behind} a reattaching subscriber issues \texttt{pullup} and adopts
the newer history without re-stamping.

The serving relay \emph{seats} the subscriber, replays the cache delta
above \texttt{since} (chunked at 96\,KB per deliver), replays all live
tombstones (unconditionally --- a since-gate cannot express ``you missed
an old deletion''), and answers with a deliver whose \texttt{from} field
\emph{pins} the subscriber: subsequent renewals go \texttt{via} the pin.
Renewal is adaptive: 5\,s while unpinned or freshly re-pinned, backing
off $\times$1.5 per stable renewal to a 60\,s ceiling; any re-pin or
upstream death snaps it back to fast. Subscribers are evicted after
180\,s without renewal.

\subsection{The tree: widen before deepen}

A relay over capacity (20 direct) \emph{promotes} one in-region leaf to
a child relay and hands it a batch of up to 8 others
(\texttt{pubsub:adopt}); the child subscribes up toward the parent and
re-fans deliveries down to its own subscribers exactly once. Only when
every direct is already a child does the tree deepen (the newcomer is
delegated to the XOR-closest child). Depth grows like
$\log_{20}(\mathrm{subscribers})$.

\subsection{Publish: stamped at the root, confirmed by observation}

\texttt{pub(topic, message, \{signWith\})} builds the signed envelope
and sends \texttt{pubsub:pub \{topicId, via, json\}} toward the topic
(via the current root hint when warm). At the root: verify the
signature (B-4), check freshness (C-2), recompute the topicId from the
signed descriptor and reject a mismatch, enforce \texttt{write:'owner'},
dedup by msgId and tombstone --- then \textbf{stamp}:
\texttt{publishTs = max(lastTs+1, now)} (strictly monotonic; the total
order) and \texttt{seq = ++role.seq} (dense per-topic counter; a gap in
delivered \texttt{seq} tells a subscriber it missed something). The
stamped message is cached, fanned to subscribers, delivered locally,
and eagerly replicated to the cohort.

\textbf{There is no publish acknowledgment, by design}: a PUB carries no
return address (publisher-location privacy). Confirmation is
\emph{observation} --- the publisher stops retrying when it sees its own
msgId arrive by any path (own delivery, own root ingest, cohort echo).
Until then the pending-publish machinery re-sends the same envelope
toward the current root hint each tick, bounded by 30\,s and 6 tries;
idempotent throughout because roots dedup by msgId. Cold publishers
(fewer than 8 neighbours) front-load extra re-sends
(\S\ref{sec:timing}) --- each send both integrates the newcomer and gets
a fresh shot at the true root.

En route, a relay holding a beacon for a closer root \emph{forwards}
the PUB rather than deferring to it: the send resolves to a verdict
before any local state moves, and only a \texttt{consumed} verdict from
the named root re-pins anything (\S\ref{sec:roots}, \emph{Writes
forward on receipts}). A publish handed to a corpse must teach the
sender something; before 4.59.0 it taught nothing and took the
sender's routing state with it.

\subsection{Convergence: one operation, many policies}

Everything that moves topic data in this system is \emph{one operation}
wearing different policy: $\mathrm{sync}(a, b, T)$ --- \textbf{make two
nodes' views of the topic's stamped set converge}. A view is summarized
by four numbers already carried on the wire: message count, high-water
(\texttt{hw}, newest stamp), low-water (\texttt{lw}, oldest stamp), and
tombstone count. Two views whose summaries match exchange nothing ---
the universal quench. The transfer rides the existing verbs
(\texttt{deliver}, \texttt{pullup}/\texttt{replayup},
\texttt{replicate}, \texttt{handoff}), and the ingest side is a single
function regardless of which verb delivered the bytes: re-verify every
author signature (B-4), dedup by msgId, apply tombstones \emph{before}
bodies (I-8), never re-stamp (a timestamp once assigned is never
reassigned), and yield to the event loop every 16 messages so bulk
adoption cannot starve liveness (I-11).

The policies --- who syncs with whom, and when:

\begin{table*}[h]
\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lllll@{}}
\toprule
Policy & Between & Trigger & Moves & Quench / bound \\
\midrule
fan-out & root $\to$ subscribers & on stamp & the new message & exactly-once dedup \\
replay-on-seat & relay $\to$ subscriber & seat / renewal & since-floor delta + all live dels & since floor \\
replay-up (hw) & child $\to$ root & SUB says $hw > \mathrm{mine}$ & the newer delta & hw catches up \\
split-union (lw) & child $\to$ root & SUB says $lw < \mathrm{mine}$ & child's full range & once per (child, lw) \\
empty-root probe & cohort+path $\to$ root & root born empty & any history & first ingest; $\le$3 tries \\
cohort replication & root $\to$ 2 closest & change / new member / 60\,s & full state, else \emph{keepalive} & signature match \\
handoff & leaver $\to$ heir & \texttt{leave()} & full state & \textsc{ack}; 2 rounds + 1 fallback \\
union-at-root & root $\gets$ any root & REPLICATE arrives & the sender's state & idempotent merge \\
read-repair & cohort $\to$ stuck subscriber & renewals stall, holder degraded & since-floor delta & first ingest \\
pull & replica $\to$ reader & on demand & one message & one answer \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table*}

Four of these policies were bought by named incidents and their reasons
are normative:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Split-union (lw)} --- after a root transition the timeline
  splits: the new root holds the post-transition half, the demoted heir
  the pre-transition half, invisible to the hw rule because it sits
  entirely \emph{below} the new root's high-water. The child advertises
  its low-water; a root seeing older history pulls the full range and
  unions it. \emph{One-shot per (child, lw)}: a refused pull (the
  child's oldest is tombstoned here, or beyond retention) must not
  re-fire on every renewal --- unguarded, this re-pulled a full cache
  every 5\,s and storm-flapped the testnet relays (4.22.0).
\item \textbf{Empty-root probe} --- a cold subscriber whose SUB
  terminates at itself becomes a root with an \emph{empty} cache while
  a live holder one hop away has everything; nothing reliably tells the
  holder about the new closer root, so the emptiness is sticky. The
  fix inverts the flow: an empty root \emph{pulls}
  (\texttt{pullup sinceHw:0}) from its cohort and its own lookup path
  (the runner-up closest is usually the prior holder), bounded at 3
  tries and quenched by the first ingest. This was 82\% of the field
  read-misses (4.24.0).
\item \textbf{Delta-gated replication} --- the cohort push sends full
  state only when the summary signature changed, a new cohort member
  needs seeding, or a 60\,s anti-entropy backstop elapses; every other
  tick sends an \emph{empty keepalive} that refreshes the backup's
  liveness clock and nothing else. The earlier behavior --- full
  cache+tombstones to the whole cohort \emph{every tick} --- reads as
  cheap idempotent anti-entropy and is exactly what this document used
  to claim; under a role-bloat storm it was the bandwidth fuel that
  collapsed the backbone (4.24.1). Convergence is unharmed: any
  divergence changes the diverged root's own signature and re-arms one
  full push.
\item \textbf{Acknowledged handoff} --- \S\ref{sec:lifecycle}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The principal-liveness rule} (normative; the generalization the
collapse paid for):

\begin{quote}
Standing state on another node may be planted only by a principal
alive to maintain it. \texttt{replicate} creates a durable
relationship --- the receiver becomes a BACKUP whose obligations run
until evicted --- so \emph{a departing node never sends
\texttt{replicate}}: it transfers ownership (\texttt{handoff}) or does
nothing. Every new sync policy must state which nature (\S above) it
creates on the receiving node and which eviction path applies; a
policy that cannot name both is rejected in review.
\end{quote}

Replayed stamps more than 5 minutes in the future are dropped (the
bad-clock rule). Caches are bounded (1024 messages / 16\,MB per role)
and expire at 24\,h from stamp. On root churn, \emph{election is just
subscription}: backups renew toward the topic every tick like any
child, so the closest backup's renewal terminates at itself and it
promotes with the history already in hand --- the same probe-protected
machinery every subscriber uses, never a bespoke local vote.

\subsection{Kill: creator-only retraction}

\texttt{kill(topic, msgId)} routes the signed kill like a publish (same
hint, same pending-retry). The root verifies the kill's own signature,
then authorship: if the target is cached, \texttt{signerPubkey} must
match the target's author \emph{now}; if not cached (kill raced its
target), the kill is accepted \emph{provisionally} --- the tombstone
records the signer, and when the target arrives, a mismatch
\emph{revokes} the tombstone and accepts the message. Kills are stamped
like publishes (they occupy a \texttt{seq} slot), fanned as
\texttt{del} markers, replicated eagerly to the cohort, and replayed on
every renewal while live. A subscriber that never received the body gets
no retraction callback (nothing to retract); the tombstone still
converges.

\subsection{Pull, metrics, host}

\texttt{pull(topic, msgId?)} is a one-shot read answered by the
\emph{first replica} the routed request reaches (by-msgId is exact by
content address; pull-latest is ``recent, not necessarily newest'' ---
hot read paths spread across the cohort instead of hammering the root).
A pull that returns nothing says \emph{which} nothing: a responder
answered and holds nothing, nobody answered in time, or a reply arrived
and would not parse. These used to collapse into one null, and every
consumer above manufactured a confident negative from it --- one such
null cost a full day of misdiagnosis against a channel that was serving
19 subscribers throughout (4.55.0). Three different facts deserve three
different values. \texttt{metrics} are demand-driven: a
\texttt{metricson} routed like a SUB arms a renewable 70\,s lease at
the root, which then publishes signed snapshots
\texttt{\{topic, ts, by, current\_count, seq, subscribers, bytes\}} to
the derived topic \texttt{metricTopic(T)} every 20\,s while the lease is
fresh --- no lease, no load; leases survive root promotion via path
flags. \texttt{host(topic?)} makes infrastructure carry without
consuming: a hosted topic is renewed like a subscription (so the node
can root it and its cache is durable) but nothing is delivered to the
application; no-arg \texttt{host()} volunteers for the node's whole
keyspace neighbourhood (retaining any role it wins as terminal) --- the
relay fleet's default mode.

% IX. ROOT MANAGEMENT (THE DECISION TABLE)
% =====================================================================
\section{Root Management}\label{sec:roots}

The root is emergent --- the live peer XOR-closest to the topic id,
discovered by routing --- so root management is a \emph{convergence}
protocol, not an election: wrong claims must yield to right ones,
quickly, without flapping, and never to a node that is farther, dead, or
departing. Since kernel 4.20.0 every \texttt{isRoot} transition passes
through one state machine (\texttt{rootClaim.js}) with one structured
log line per flip.\marginnote{The companion note \emph{Root Management
--- the RootClaim state machine} gives the full treatment with worked
examples; this section is the normative summary.}

\subsection{Transitions}

\begin{table*}[h]
\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Transition & why-code & Trigger \\
\midrule
become & \texttt{sub/pub/kill/metricson-terminal} & topic message terminated here, no role \\
become & \texttt{handoff-heir} & departing root handed us history \\
promote & \texttt{terminal-promote} & non-root relay became bare-topic terminal \\
demote & \texttt{defer-terminal} & stranded traffic deferred to beaconed root \\
demote & \texttt{beacon-closer} & beacon proved strictly-closer live root \\
demote & \texttt{verify-closer} & self-verification found closer terminus \\
demote & \texttt{handoff-better-heir} & post-handoff closer live root ($\ne$ leaver) \\
claim & \texttt{reachable-fallback} & deferral unconfirmed 6\,s; self closest reachable \\
adopt & \texttt{adopted-child} & ADOPT made us a non-root child \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table*}

Every demotion does three things atomically as policy: flip
\texttt{isRoot}, pin upstream to the winner, and send a confirming
subscribe (the winner must \emph{adopt} us or our subtree starves).

\subsection{The defer gate (never yield to farther, ghost, or leaver)}

Before any claim, \texttt{liveCloserRoot(topic)} consults the cached
root beacon for the topic. Rules, in order: no fresh beacon
$\rightarrow$ claim; beacon names self or a node \emph{not strictly
closer} $\rightarrow$ claim (the cardinal rule --- also the security
property: a lying beacon cannot divert traffic outward); otherwise
liveness evidence is required, strongest first: a \texttt{verified}
pointer (network-confirmed by iterative lookup, TTL 90\,s), a
channel-verified direct neighbour (opens \emph{instantly} on churn ---
the dead root's channel drop is the gate), or a beacon heard within
$1.5\times$\texttt{BEACON\_MS} = 30\,s (a live root re-beacons every
20\,s; silence is death). The freshness cut applies to \emph{every}
form of beacon evidence, verified pointers included. It did not always:
until 4.59.0 a \texttt{verified} pointer bypassed the cut, so one stale
pointer to a dead root could steer traffic at a corpse for as long as
the pointer lived. SUB, METRICSON, promotion, and post-handoff use the
strict mode: seating infrastructure demands channel or network
evidence.

\subsection{Writes forward on receipts, not on faith}

Reads and writes used to pass different gates, and the asymmetry cost a
production outage. On 2026-08-02 a host restart took out most of a
relay fleet; reads kept working for the surviving holders, because
\texttt{\_onSub} demanded channel evidence before deferring --- but
\texttt{\_onPub} and \texttt{\_onKill} deferred to any beaconed root
with no liveness test at all. One stale beacon ate every write to its
slice for hours while six of the eight holders sat alive and willing.
The read path checked its evidence; the write path trusted a pointer.

Since 4.59.0 a PUB or KILL toward a beaconed root is a
\emph{forward}, not a defer, and the distinction is load-bearing:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dispatch first, mutate on receipt.} The old defer demoted
  the local role and re-pinned upstream \emph{before} sending --- so a
  publish toward a dead relay both vanished and left the sender wired
  to the corpse. Now nothing about local role or upstream state moves
  until the dispatch verdict resolves \texttt{consumed} \emph{and}
  \texttt{atNode} names the root the forward was aimed at. A failed
  send moves nothing; the sender's own retries then route by the
  ordinary rules.
\item \textbf{A failed verdict revokes only what it tested.} A forward
  that fails invalidates the one beacon record it was built on ---
  matched by root identity \emph{and} capture time --- and nothing
  else. A verdict that captured no record has no deletion authority: it
  describes a probe of a pointer the node never held, and a
  newer-generation beacon that arrived mid-flight survives it
  (4.59.2). Without the strict match, one slow failure could erase the
  fresh pointer that had just replaced the stale one.
\end{itemize}

Both halves are fenced (\texttt{fence\_pub\_defers\_to\_corpse}, red
against the pre-fix kernel, green after), and measured live: on the
testnet fleet, writes issued 5 seconds after a root's SIGKILL --- inside
the freshness window, the exact regime that ate the 2026-08-02 writes
--- delivered 30 of 30, in each of two runs against different victims.
Writes at the 30\,s boundary the criterion actually demands: also 30 of
30.

\subsection{The evidence planes}

\textbf{Beacons}: every root advertises \texttt{\{root, topics[]\}} to
its 6 XOR-closest neighbours, forwarded 2 layers (basin $\approx$ 42),
every 20\,s and immediately on becoming root; pointer TTL 50\,s;
receivers apply verify-don't-trust (accept only if at least as close as
the best locally-known node). A wrong claimant hearing a strictly-closer
beacon demotes at once --- which also silences its own poisoning
beacons. \textbf{Self-verification}: beacons only reach the basin, so
every root re-checks its own claim with the same iterative lookup
subscribers use --- at 6\,s after forming (the fresh-topic race window),
then every 45\,s, batched 3 per tick, never awaited inside the tick.
Finding a strictly-closer live node seeds a \texttt{verified} pointer
and demotes. This whole plane depends on the DHT adapter returning a
real \texttt{LookupResult}: the default adapter used to return a bare
id while every consumer read \texttt{r.path}, so self-verification and
the iterative escapes were silent no-ops on \emph{every} standalone
peer --- present in the code, absent from the network --- until 4.28.1. \textbf{Death sweeps}: when a peer's channel closes or it
announces leave, every beacon naming it \emph{and every upstream pin on
it} is purged, with the pinned subscription's renewal clock reset ---
re-homing is next-tick, and the reachable-root fallback re-arms.
\textbf{The replica ledger}: \texttt{role.replicas} is durability
bookkeeping --- it decides whether a topic counts as safely replicated
and whether history survives the root leaving --- and it records a
backup only on a \emph{verified dispatch}: the replicate resolved
\texttt{consumed} at the named node. It used to record on the strength
of a local call that had not thrown, and routing does not throw --- it
reports failure by resolving. Measured before the 4.57.0 fix: thirteen
replicate sends, all failed, ledger reading \texttt{replicas:\,2}. A
ledger written from intentions is not a ledger.

\subsection{The fallback asymmetry}

A subscriber unpinned for 6\,s despite renewals (its hint names a
closer-but-unreachable node) claims the root itself if it is closest
among \emph{reachable} peers: a reachable root beats a closer
unconfirmed one. Claiming wrongly is cheap --- beacons or verification
demote it within one period; deferring wrongly strands the topic
forever. When the mesh is bare (no non-bridge neighbours), a self-SUB
terminal claims nothing --- ``terminal at self'' on an unmeshed node is
isolation, not closeness; the seat is held and the fast renewal re-runs
the decision once meshed. Publish-side is not gated, by design: a solo
node still roots its own publish.

% X. LIFECYCLE: JOIN AND LEAVE
% =====================================================================
\section{Lifecycle: Join and Leave}\label{sec:lifecycle}

\textbf{Join}: \texttt{connect(\{bridge, identity?\})} performs the
bridge bootstrap of \S\ref{sec:transport}, resolves the directory,
\emph{self-integrates}, and returns a started peer. There is no second
call to make and no hidden step --- \texttt{connect()} is the single,
complete entry point (4.40.0), and the completeness was bought the hard
way. Self-integration --- \texttt{findKClosest(self)} plus opened,
authenticated channels to the results, the primitive that lifts a
newcomer from the passive-adoption floor (sim-validated: 7--27\%
reachability without it, 95--98\% with) --- used to run only on the
sponsored \texttt{join(sponsor)} path. The two one-call paths every
real application uses, \texttt{connect()} and no-sponsor
\texttt{join()}, both skipped it (4.39.0). So applications sat at the
churn floor and self-rooted their topics as singletons in sparse
regions: fresh subscribers read nothing, publishers stranded on leave
--- and the one application that worked, worked only because it called
the integration primitive by hand. An entry point that requires a
manual step to work is not an entry point. Reachability still ripens
over the first seconds as inbound traffic writes the newcomer into
neighbours' tables; the cold publisher burst and the fast renewal floor
are shaped around exactly this window.

\textbf{Leave} (\texttt{leave(\{timeoutMs\})}) is a contract with three
promises, each bought by an incident --- and the \emph{order} of the
promises is itself the contract. \texttt{leave()} used to announce the
departure first. Receivers hard-close the leaver's channels on that
signal (the proactive re-anchor fast path), so every routed
\texttt{handoff} that followed found no route, terminated back at the
sender, and was silently discarded --- captured live as 173 of 173
leave handoffs boomeranging with zero acks. Every topic whose only copy
rode a handoff died with the leaver, deterministically, about 10\% of
publishes. The courtesy call announced the death before the will was
read. The order is now drain, hand off, \emph{then} notify (4.32.0),
and no promise below may be reordered past another:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Drain on evidence}: poll until the pending publish/kill maps
  are empty --- a confirmed publish departs instantly, an unconfirmed one
  gets the caller's full window (never a hidden cap). Internal waits are
  ref'd timers: Node must not exit mid-leave.
\item \emph{Hand off every last copy, confirmed}: for each cache-bearing
  topic the leaver ROOTS \emph{or holds as a BACKUP}, resolve an heir
  \emph{and a runner-up} --- in-region first (an out-of-region holder is
  durable but unfindable by routed reads), local K-closest, falling
  back to the \emph{iterative} lookup when the leaver's table is thin
  (the fresh burst-publisher case) --- with 8 resolutions in flight,
  singleton roots first, the whole handoff bounded in proportion to the
  role count (cap 60\,s). Rooted-only handoff was the
  \textsc{handoff\_gap}: under churn a copy cascades root
  $\to$ backup, the backup departs, and the last copy dies with a node
  that never considered it worth handing off --- 100\% of a
  field-measured 9--13\% restart loss classified as exactly this
  (4.31.0). Then send \texttt{pubsub:handoff \{topicId, msgs, dels,
  from\}} in \emph{acknowledged rounds}: all unacked heirs per round, a
  shared 700\,ms ack window scaled under load, two rounds. The heir
  replies \texttt{handoffack} --- and the ack claims only what the heir
  \emph{holds}. The ingest path had five silent early-returns, and the
  ack said \texttt{\{topicId\}} regardless, so an heir that rejected
  half a batch acked byte-identically to one that took all of it; the
  leaver then exempted the topic from every retry and departed with the
  rejected half (4.45.0). Fire-and-forget was worse still: it dropped a
  topic's last copy whenever the one send didn't land (40/40
  confirmation failures in the field). A topic still unacked after the
  rounds gets \emph{one} fallback \texttt{handoff} to the runner-up ---
  worst case two proper holders whose caches union --- and \emph{never}
  a \texttt{replicate}: acks are a race against load, so ``unacked''
  usually means ``acked late,'' and a departing node spraying replica
  state plants backups of a dead principal (the principal-liveness
  rule; violating it collapsed the backbone twice). \texttt{from} names
  the leaver so the heir purges the leaver's ghost beacon and never
  defers its new claim back to it.
\item \emph{Then silence}: notify peers in parallel (they run the death
  sweep immediately), stop the tick, clear all retry state. A peer that
  has left sends nothing --- and the network stops listening for it.
\end{enumerate}

Abrupt death is the same story without the courtesy: channels drop,
neighbours sweep beacons and pins instantly, the warm cohort holds the
history, and election-by-subscription seats the successor.

repair: subscription renewals (adaptive), hosted re-announces, backup

subscriber eviction and cache/tombstone expiry, role teardown, beacon
emission, and the self-verification batch. The only timers outside the
tick are the cold-publish burst and the one-shot first-publish re-send

\subsection{Behavioural invariants --- what must always hold}

Each entry closes with its fence and, where one exists, the identifier
it carried in \texttt{INVARIANTS.md}.

\begingroup\sloppy
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{I-1}] A topic \emph{converges} to a single root, and wrong
  claims converge without flapping. This is a liveness property, not
  mutual exclusion: duplicate roots are an expected transient, and the
  system contains no distributed lock that would forbid them. Two bounds
  qualify it. Reconciliation reaches only as far as
  \texttt{rootReplicas} (currently~2), so a second root outside that
  reach is not repaired by reconciliation and persists until churn
  removes it. And under partition each side converges independently and
  stamps its own dense sequence; the merge on heal is a union, not an
  ordering. An implementation must converge and must not flap; it must
  not assume an exclusivity it does not have. Three sub-rules carry it:
  a joiner iteratively verifies past its local table before self-rooting
  (\emph{was B2}); a root union-ingests a \texttt{REPLICATE} rather than
  usurping or refusing it (\emph{was B4}); and an empty self-root pulls
  cohort history before acting as root (\emph{was B9}).\\
  {\footnotesize\textsf{Fence:} \texttt{smoke\_root\_hint} $\cdot$
  \texttt{smoke\_interloper\_convergence} $\cdot$
  \texttt{smoke\_split\_history\_union} $\cdot$
  \texttt{smoke\_empty\_root\_pull}}
\item[\textbf{I-2}] Never defer a root claim to a farther node, a
  ghost, or the node handing off.\\
  {\footnotesize\textsf{Fence:} \texttt{smoke\_leave\_handoff\_burst}
  (heir no-defer case) $\cdot$ \emph{was B3}}
\item[\textbf{I-3}] A recovery path never waits unboundedly on --- or
  dies on --- the failure it handles.\\
  {\footnotesize\textbf{UNFENCED.} Issue \#339 ---
  \texttt{transport.start()} must fail loud.}
\item[\textbf{I-4}] A peer that has left is silent, and its rooted
  history departs before it does. Handoff completes \emph{before}
  notify --- data first, funeral announcement second (\emph{was B5}) ---
  a departing non-root holder hands off unless the root is positively
  alive, since passive freshness lies during mass teardown (\emph{was
  B6}), and a mass leaver's sole-copy topics go first, singletons before
  replicated roots before holders (\emph{was B11}).\\
  {\footnotesize\textsf{Fence:} \texttt{smoke\_leave\_teardown} $\cdot$
  \texttt{smoke\_pubsub\_leave\_handoff} $\cdot$
  \texttt{smoke\_backup\_handoff} $\cdot$
  \texttt{smoke\_handoff\_scaling} (tier order only indirectly ---
  \textbf{partial})}
\item[\textbf{I-5}] A client is never judged by time the server wasn't
  listening.\\
  {\footnotesize\textbf{UNFENCED} at the kernel; the bridge's
  hello-timeout second strike is issue \#338.}
\item[\textbf{I-6}] Observability surfaces exist or fail loudly ---
  never silently zero.\\
  {\footnotesize\textbf{UNFENCED.} Metrics were silently dead across all
  of 4.x before anyone noticed; a metric name that does not match its
  referent is the same defect (\texttt{axona\_status} calls the
  synaptome \texttt{mesh}, issue \#411). No operator surface exposes
  live channel count, so ``did the mesh survive?'' cannot be answered
  from outside.}
\item[\textbf{I-7}] Fixes must hold for 100\%-transient peers: no
  mechanism may privilege stable nodes to mask churn.\\
  {\footnotesize\textbf{UNFENCED}, and unfenceable by a unit test ---
  this one is enforced at review. Stability-weighted root election was
  proposed, simulated, and rejected under it.}
\item[\textbf{I-8}] Migrated cache never resurrects a killed message:
  every migration path carries tombstones, applied first. A kill
  callback fires only if the body reached that app (\emph{was B8}).\\
  {\footnotesize\textsf{Fence:} \texttt{smoke\_kill\_migration} $\cdot$
  \texttt{smoke\_pubsub\_kill} $\cdot$ \emph{was B7}}
\item[\textbf{I-9}] Publish confirmation is observation, not
  acknowledgment; nothing may add an ack channel that discloses the
  publisher's location.\\
  {\footnotesize\textbf{UNFENCED} behaviourally; enforced by the
  envelope's shape --- it names a signer and carries no node id and no
  region. Two attempts to add one were reverted.}
\item[\textbf{I-10}] Standing state is bounded by demand, never by
  churn history: per-topic state anywhere is $O(\mathrm{subscribers} +
  \mathrm{cohort})$; no mechanism may create state that accumulates
  with join/leave \emph{events}; every write of standing state onto
  another node names its eviction path in the same change. Corollary:
  the principal-liveness rule (\S\ref{sec:pubsub}) --- a planted nature
  is retired only when its principal is gone \emph{and} re-homed.\\
  {\footnotesize\textsf{Fence:} \texttt{smoke\_sync\_engine} (evictor
  column complete for every policy row) $\cdot$ \emph{was B10}}
\item[\textbf{I-11}] Bulk work never starves liveness: unbounded batch
  ingest/emission yields to the event loop at a fixed stride; a node is
  never evicted by its peers \emph{because} it was absorbing history.\\
  {\footnotesize\textbf{UNFENCED} as a property; the pacing constants
  are in the timing table (\S\ref{sec:timing}) and the join-storm that
  motivated it is issue \#332.}
\item[\textbf{I-12}] A write is never handed to a root without live
  evidence --- a channel, a network-verified pointer, or a beacon
  fresher than $1.5\times$\texttt{BEACON\_MS} --- and local routing
  state mutates only on a \texttt{consumed} verdict from the node the
  write was aimed at. The read path and the write path pass the same
  gate.\\
  {\footnotesize\textbf{UNFENCED} on the write path --- issue \#422
  records a \texttt{PUB} deferring to an unreachable root. The read side
  is covered by \texttt{smoke\_ghost\_read}.}
\item[\textbf{I-13}] Durable bookkeeping is written from receipts,
  never from sends. A replica, an upstream pin, a beacon invalidation
  --- each requires a verdict naming the node it concerns; a send that
  merely didn't throw proves nothing, because routing reports failure
  by resolving.\\
  {\footnotesize\textbf{UNFENCED.} The general shape --- a send whose
  non-throw was read as delivery --- has appeared three times.}
\item[\textbf{I-14}] A negative result names its kind. Timeout,
  answered-empty, and unparseable are three facts; any surface that
  collapses them into one value invites its caller to manufacture a
  confident wrong conclusion.\\
  {\footnotesize\textbf{UNFENCED.} Issue \#418 is a live instance: a
  one-shot subscribe reads zero on a topic a standing watch is holding
  traffic on, and zero is indistinguishable from empty.}
\item[\textbf{I-15}] Transport identity is ephemeral; author identity
  is durable. A node's \texttt{nodeId} and its keypair are minted fresh
  every process start and written to no persistent store --- not a
  namespace, not a file, not a snapshot, not a container volume. An
  author identity may and must persist. This one protects people rather
  than correctness: a \texttt{nodeId} surviving restart is a durable
  correlator linking a node's sessions to each other, to an IP, and so
  to a person, and the countervailing benefit is nil, because a node
  returning under its old id gains nothing from a mesh that has already
  healed around its absence. Reconnection continuity, route retention,
  log correlation, deduplicating concurrent processes, and node-keyed
  reputation are each a signal the design is wrong, not a justification.\\
  {\footnotesize\textsf{Fence:} \texttt{smoke\_persistence\_wiring}
  (restart $\Rightarrow$ different \texttt{nodeId}, author survives)
  $\cdot$ \texttt{smoke\_snapshot} $\cdot$
  \texttt{fence\_transport\_identity} (static, one per repo) $\cdot$
  \emph{was I-ID}}
\item[\textbf{I-16}] Region is an optimization, never a wall. A region
  prefix biases \emph{where} a topic prefers to live; it never decides
  whether a node may hold, root, or route one, and a sparse region must
  roll over into its neighbours. The failure mode of a missed
  optimization is a slower hop; the failure mode of an enforced wall is
  data with nowhere to live. The refusal machinery exists and is dormant
  behind \texttt{isRegionLockEnforced === false} --- \emph{leaving it off
  is the invariant}, which is the opposite of what a reader would
  conclude from the flag's existence. Ranking, mint-point choice, and
  holder preference may depend on region; admission and capability may
  not.\\
  {\footnotesize\textsf{Fence:} \texttt{smoke\_region\_lock} covers the
  dormant path only --- \textbf{no fence asserts the default stays
  off}. Treating the prefix as a wall once produced 0\% cross-region
  delivery (4.17.1). $\cdot$ \emph{was B1}}
\item[\textbf{I-17}] A bridge is a bridge --- it transports and
  introduces, and holds no topic role at any load, in any topology, with
  no floor exception. Three independent doors enforce it: routing skips
  it in the next-hop scan, it does not \texttt{host()}, and
  \texttt{neverRoot} makes its own \texttt{sub()} unable to self-root at
  a tier the admission floor may not override. The tempting exception
  --- let it root when it is the only candidate --- would have it root
  exactly when it can least afford to, and buys only a few seconds of
  empty-network durability during a fresh launch.\\
  {\footnotesize\textsf{Fence:} \texttt{smoke\_role\_admission} (refusal
  is HARD; the floor must not override) --- \textbf{no fence asserts a
  bridge peer ends bring-up with \texttt{axonRoles} empty}. $\cdot$
  \emph{was B12}}
\item[\textbf{I-18}] Capacity is measured, never counted. Fitness to
  hold roles is observed pressure against real protocol deadlines ---
  service staleness against \texttt{DROP\_MS}, tick lag against
  \texttt{HELLO\_DEADLINE\_MS} --- never \texttt{axonRoles.size}. A
  count is inventory; pressure is capability, and capability is what
  predicts failure. A node holding the maximum roles and servicing all
  of them on time is a healthy node.\\
  {\footnotesize\textsf{Fence:} \texttt{smoke\_role\_admission} $\cdot$
  \emph{was B13}. Caveat in the same breath: \texttt{servicePressure}
  once sat pinned at 0.028 while a node held 546 roles at 200\% CPU, so
  the measure is right in principle and has been wrong in practice ---
  a metric that does not match its referent is I-6's problem.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Process rules --- how changes land}

Not properties of the running system, so not numbered with the
invariants; they are here because every one was bought with a bad day.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Gates are pre-registered, not post-hoc.} The SLO is
  fresh-subscriber delivery percentage. A measurement is REPS $\ge 5$
  reported as mean $\pm$ sd; a single run is directional only.
\item \textbf{No behaviour change without a disproof.} A failing repro
  precedes every fix, and the repro becomes the fence. This is why the
  UNFENCED entries above are unfenced --- they were reasoned to, not
  reproduced.
\item \textbf{Mechanism freeze during refactor phases.} Incident
  response is a revert or a minimal guard carrying an issue ID and a
  removal date. No new mechanisms.
\item \textbf{A mixed-version compatibility statement} is required for
  any change touching wire-adjacent behaviour. Production runs several
  kernel versions at once --- 4.35 peers were observed during 4.41.
\item \textbf{Read deployed versions from the running service.} Any
  version used to size, gate, or justify work comes from
  \texttt{/healthz} at the time of writing, never from a note, a memory,
  or a previous document. A release was once sized as four versions of
  new subsystem when it was four bugfixes, and the error survived a full
  review pass because every reader trusted the same note.
\item \textbf{Verify per claim, not per narrative.} A review checks each
  numeric claim against source. A plausible story carries false figures
  through unchallenged.
\end{itemize}

% XIII. ROBUSTNESS: WHAT FAILS, AND WHAT HAPPENS
% =====================================================================
\section{Robustness: What Fails, and What Happens}\label{sec:robustness}

\begin{description}
\item[A subscriber's relay dies.] Neighbour case: death sweep drops the
  pin, renewal snaps fast, re-home next tick. Otherwise: the next
  renewal routes toward the corpse, pops at the live terminal, re-seats
  at the true root, and the deliver re-pins --- worst case one renewal
  interval.
\item[The topic-closest node is alive but useless.] Dead is the easy
  case --- the channel drops and everything sweeps. A \emph{degraded}
  holder answers the transport and fails the protocol, so nothing
  sweeps and a subscriber can renew into it forever. Read escalation
  (4.33.0) probes past dead \emph{and} degraded holders, and
  stuck-subscriber read-repair (4.36.0) lets the cohort push the
  since-floor delta to a subscriber whose renewals stall against a
  holder that will not serve --- quenched on first ingest, one row in
  the policy table. The A/B under packet loss is the receipt: 4.36
  against 4.35, same loss rate, read-repair carried the difference.
  The bridge departure hints that shipped alongside are marked
  TEMPORARY in the source and carry a reachability guard; a permanent
  mechanism must not lean on the bridge (\S\ref{sec:transport}).
\item[The root dies abruptly.] Its beacons are swept by neighbours; the
  warm cohort already holds cache+tombstones; the closest backup's next
  renewal terminates at itself and it promotes, gap-free; the rest
  re-home under it. Singleton topics survive because replication is
  proactive, not reactive. Writes in flight ride the forward contract:
  a send at the corpse fails to a verdict, the sender's pending retry
  re-routes, and the stale beacon is cut at $1.5\times$ the beacon
  period even if nothing sweeps it sooner. Measured on the live fleet:
  30 of 30 writes issued 5\,s after a SIGKILL delivered, twice, against
  different victims.
\item[A stale beacon names a corpse.] The pre-4.59 failure mode, and
  the one that cost 2026-08-02's production writes: the pointer
  outlives the node, and everything routed by it vanishes while the
  node's neighbours still answer reads. Now every beacon path ---
  verified pointers included --- carries the 30\,s freshness cut, a
  forward that fails revokes exactly the pointer it tested, and no
  verdict deletes a pointer it never captured. The corpse's influence
  over writes ends with its beacon's freshness, not with luck.
\item[A send fails without a sound.] Routing does not throw ---
  \texttt{routeMessage} reports failure by \emph{resolving}
  \texttt{\{consumed:\,false\}} --- so a caller that discards the
  promise cannot tell delivery from silence, and no try/catch will ever
  tell it. The containment lives in one place: every emission path
  returns the verdict, a transport error becomes a failure verdict of
  the same shape, and a rejecting transport cannot kill the process
  (4.57.1). Callers that ignore the result stay safe; callers that read
  it get the truth. The 4.49.0 class --- a node dead in every duty while
  its health endpoint reads fine, bought by one swallowed
  \texttt{TypeError} in the repair tick --- is fenced against
  recurrence.
\item[The root leaves gracefully.] Handoff pushes history to heirs
  (parallel, timeout-raced, thin-table lookup fallback); heirs never
  defer back to the leaver.
\item[A publish strands.] The greedy walk hit a local minimum; the
  pending retry re-sends toward the refreshed hint each tick (bounded);
  cold publishers burst. The retrying is idempotent end-to-end.
\item[A cold reader becomes an empty root.] Its SUB terminated at
  itself while a live holder kept the history --- the sticky read-miss
  class. The birth probe pulls from the cohort and the lookup path
  within a second; the tick re-probes while empty, bounded at 3; the
  first ingest quenches. The reader serves history, not emptiness.
\item[A burst publisher churns out under load.] Its \texttt{leave()}
  hands off in acknowledged rounds; acks lost to load trigger one
  fallback handoff to the runner-up --- never a replica spray. Worst
  case two holders whose caches union; the fleet's role population is
  unchanged (I-10).
\item[History arrives in bulk.] A joiner adopting thousands of cached
  messages yields to its event loop every 16; heartbeats interleave;
  its mesh peers do not evict it for absorbing what they sent it
  (I-11).
\item[Two roots exist transiently.] Near-miss terminals can claim while
  evidence is thin --- by design. Beacons reconcile within the basin;
  self-verification reconciles across it; cohort anti-entropy keeps the
  data unified while the claims converge; a since-all rejoin reads the
  union.
\item[The network partitions.] Each side converges internally (roots,
  cohorts). On heal, beacons and verification collapse duplicate roots;
  anti-entropy unions the caches; per-publisher \texttt{seq} lets
  readers order interleaved histories.
\item[The bridge restarts.] Existing mesh connections are unaffected
  (the bridge is signaling-only). Reconnecting peers ride through
  transient 502s --- the reconnect path survives the very errors
  reconnection produces (I-3) --- and re-establish. Nothing about topic
  state lives on the bridge.
\item[A peer lies.] A forged envelope fails B-4 verification at the
  root; a forged kill fails authorship and is revoked even if
  provisionally recorded; a lying beacon cannot point farther than the
  receiver's best-known node; gossip cannot write routing tables; a
  claimed nodeId must hash from the proven key.
\item[Clocks are wrong.] Stamps are root-monotonic regardless of wall
  clocks; replayed stamps too far ahead are dropped; freshness gates use
  bounded skew.
\end{description}

% XIII-B. THE SHIP OF THESEUS
% =====================================================================
\section{The Ship of Theseus: Durability Under Replacement}\label{sec:theseus}

Replace every plank of a ship, one at a time, and ask whether the same
ship remains. Replace every node of an Axona network, one at a time,
and the question stops being philosophy: does the data survive? The
preceding section lists the mechanisms; this one reports what happens
when a 200-node fleet is replaced end to end, node by node, with the
mechanisms doing their work. The numbers are from the theseus harness
(dht-sim, 200 nodes, 32 topics per run, 10 messages per topic,
replacement interval as the independent variable); single runs are
noise, so every figure is aggregated across repetitions.

\textbf{Loss comes in whole topics.} Across every arm measured --- soft
and hard, thousands of topics --- a topic read back after full
replacement is either complete or empty, never partial. The cohort
replicates full state; a survivor has everything or was never in the
cohort at all. This granularity is the fact the rest of the model
hangs on, and it is why the durability question reduces to one event:
does a topic's last warm holder die before anyone notices.

\textbf{Graceful replacement loses nothing.} With \texttt{leave()} ---
handoff before announcement, \S\ref{sec:lifecycle} --- loss was 0.000\%
across 180 runs, 1,890 topics, 60,165 messages, at every replacement
speed measured. The Ship of Theseus sails home intact, provided each
plank is removed by a carpenter and not by cannon fire.

\textbf{Abrupt replacement is a race, and the clock is the killer.}
Kill nodes with no handoff --- SIGKILL, the cannon --- and loss appears,
governed by timing rather than placement: 2.2\% of topics at a
1.2\,s replacement interval, falling roughly 30-fold as the interval
stretches 16-fold (log-log slope $-1.28$), toward zero as replacement
slows past the cohort's own re-check pace. Nodes died in random order,
so the victims scattered across the address space; kill in address
order and a topic can lose its whole cohort at once. 2.2\% is the good
case.

\textbf{Holder count is a proxy, not a cause.} Per-death, across 904
single-death topics: one warm backup lost 25.0\%, two lost 8.9\%
($\pm$2.3, n$=$616), three lost 0\% (n$=$202). Read alone, that table
says ``add a replica.'' The controlled comparison says otherwise:
raising \texttt{ROOT\_REPLICAS} from 2 to 3 roughly halved loss at the
cost of 50\% more replication traffic --- and installed no floor.
Worse for the simple story, topics holding three warm backups in the
3-replica arm still lost 11.2\% under identical churn while the
2-replica arm's three-backup topics lost nothing. The count was
standing in for something else: a \emph{quiet, well-connected
neighbourhood} whose members hear about the death in time. Durability
is not how many copies exist. It is whether the survivors' next
conversation happens before the next death.

\textbf{Communication is the substrate.} That reading was David's
conjecture --- the network is built around information flow, so a
network that talks more survives more --- and two measurements back
it. First, the renewal asymmetry: backups re-check their principal
every tick, ungated at 5\,s, while ordinary subscribers back off from
5\,s to 60\,s; the cohort talks twelve times as often as the
subscriber population, and the cohort is what survives. Second,
directed integration: a newcomer becomes reachable only when traffic
arrives \emph{at} it, writing it into its neighbours' tables --- about
4 directed packets per newcomer achieved what two and a half times as
much undirected warmup traffic did. The lever on durability is the
rate of the right conversations, not the size of the crew.

\textbf{The live fleet agrees, with a tail.} On the 26-relay testnet
fleet (kernel 4.59.2), the census reads a median of three warm holders
per topic --- root plus two backups, as configured --- but 2 of 30
freshly-minted topics sat at two holders before any churn at all, and
the tail widened to 6 of 30 under sequential kills. Six targeted
deaths (the largest role-holders, 90\,s apart) lost nothing: 30 of 30
topics replayed complete. Six deaths cannot observe a per-death rate
of a few percent on a thin tail; the sim's 904 deaths could, and did.
The thin tail is where the loss lives. Watch it in hours, not
minutes.

\textbf{What this means for operating a fleet:} call \texttt{leave()}
on every shutdown you control; roll one node at a time with the
replacement already live; treat abrupt mass death as the one event
the protocol does not yet promise to absorb, and measure it before
trusting it. The open design limit is recorded rather than hidden:
root reconciliation reaches only the \texttt{ROOT\_REPLICAS} cohort,
so a duplicate root that forms beyond it is permanent until re-rooted
--- a known limit, tracked, not yet paid for.

\section{The Application API}\label{sec:api}

The kernel's public surface on \texttt{AxonaPeer} (browser and Node
identical): lifecycle --- \texttt{connect()} / \texttt{join()},
\texttt{start()}, \texttt{leave(\{timeoutMs\})}, \texttt{stop()},
\texttt{ready()}; pub/sub --- \texttt{pub(topic, message,
\{signWith\})}, \texttt{sub(topic, \{since, replayLatest\})},
\texttt{unsub}, \texttt{kill(topic, msgId)}, \texttt{pull(topic,
msgId?)}, \texttt{metrics(topic)}, \texttt{host(topic?)},
\texttt{unhost}; identity --- \texttt{createNodeIdentity},
\texttt{createAuthorIdentity} (module exports); direct messaging ---
\texttt{send}/\texttt{notify}/\texttt{onMessage}; introspection ---
\texttt{health()} (roles, hosting, mesh truth), \texttt{peers()},
\texttt{rootedTopics()}, \texttt{onPeerJoin}/\texttt{onPeerLeave},
\texttt{onLog}/\texttt{onError}, \texttt{onUpgradeRequired};
persistence --- \texttt{snapshot()}/\texttt{fromSnapshot()} plus the
PersistenceAdapter (IndexedDB, file, memory). Topic arguments are
descriptors (\S\ref{sec:identity}); \texttt{signWith} takes an author
identity or the \texttt{ANONYMOUS} sentinel. Applications render
message bodies through \texttt{std/message} and move bulk bytes through
\texttt{std/chunk} (10\,KB chunks under the 15\,KB reliable floor;
chunked receive returns per-file msgIds so a transfer can later be
\texttt{kill}ed).

Two return-shape facts, each the residue of a confident-false-negative:
\texttt{pull} returns the \emph{full envelope} --- msgId, stamp, signer,
body --- not the bare body; the bare-body shape made every publish
confirmation built on it read \texttt{null} and fail forever (4.29.0).
And \texttt{metrics().publishes} is a real throughput counter; it was
wired to nothing and reported zero for the life of the 4.x line until
4.41.0 --- an observability surface that existed and silently lied,
the exact class I-6 forbids.

% XV. SECURITY MODEL
% =====================================================================
\section{Security Model}

Self-authentication end to end: content believed only against the
author key it carries (B-4 at every root ingress and every stamped
re-ingest); transport believed only against the node key proven on the
channel (axona/5, DTLS-fingerprint binding on WebRTC); freshness and
per-publisher monotonic \texttt{seq} against replay (C-2);
domain-tagged signatures everywhere (no cross-protocol replay); total
canonical encoding shared by signer and verifier (C-1); inbound size and
count caps on every attacker-controllable field (D-1: 256\,KB hard
publish ceiling, 15\,KB reliable floor, via $\le$ 8, beacon topics
$\le$ 256); first-party-only routing tables against eclipse (B-3);
verify-don't-trust on every hint (beacons, pull answers, replayed
history). Address grinding is answered by memory-hard proof-of-work on a
\emph{separate puzzle} $H(\mathrm{domain}\,\Vert\,\mathrm{role}\,\Vert\,
\mathrm{pubkey}\,\Vert\,\mathrm{nonce})$ --- never on the address itself,
which would skew the keyspace (calibration complete; activation gated).
The standing privacy rule bears repeating: the protocol learns
\emph{who} from envelopes and \emph{where} from node ids, and no layer
may join the two.

% XVI. PRODUCTION DEPLOYMENT

\section{Production Deployment}

Two isolated networks, partitioned hermetically by wire major.
\textbf{Production} runs three kinds of standing infrastructure, and
the doc describes their natures rather than their addresses: multiple
\emph{bridges} (Dockerized, federated by uplink, advertising on the
directory topic) spread across regions; a \emph{relay backbone} ---
groups of keyspace-hosting relays in several geographic regions, so no
single region's outage removes the majority of standing holders; and
the reference application, \texttt{axona.chat}. \textbf{Testnet} is
the staging network --- its own bridge and apps, hermetically separate
--- and every kernel release soaks there first; prod promotion is a
deliberate, evidence-gated step (full suite, external axonSpec gate,
settled-mesh soak acceptance, and the standing runbook). Every component surfaces its
\texttt{KERNEL\_VERSION} at a runtime-inspectable surface; deploys are
staggered and judged only after the mesh settle window, never by
one-shot fresh clients.

% XVII. RECONSTRUCTION GUIDE (FOR IMPLEMENTERS, HUMAN OR AI)
% =====================================================================
\section{Reconstruction Guide}\label{sec:reconstruction}

To rebuild a conformant peer from this note alone, implement in this
order, testing each layer before the next:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Canonical form and ids} (\S\ref{sec:identity}): total
  canonical JSON; SHA-256; Ed25519; the 264-bit id discipline (region
  byte, hex$\leftrightarrow$BigInt); \texttt{deriveTopicId} with its
  region-resolution rule; \texttt{msgId}. Test: same inputs, byte-equal
  outputs, cross-implementation.
\item \textbf{Envelope and kill} (\S\ref{sec:identity}): build / sign /
  verify with domain tags; freshness and seq rules. Test: a signature
  from one implementation verifies in the other; domain confusion
  fails.
\item \textbf{Routing} (\S\ref{sec:routing}): synaptome with
  first-party admission; greedy \texttt{routeMessage} with terminal
  detection and hop budget; iterative \texttt{findKClosest}; dead-peer
  skip; bridge exclusion. Test: terminal correctness on random
  topologies; lookup escapes a constructed local minimum.
\item \textbf{Transport and handshake} (\S\ref{sec:transport}):
  any reliable channel + the axona/5 mutual auth with channel binding;
  the wire-major gate. Sim transport first; real transports are
  substitutable.
\item \textbf{The routed pub/sub core} (\S\ref{sec:wire},
  \S\ref{sec:pubsub}): the topic decision (handle / reroute / forward /
  reject) with waypoint popping; roles; SUB seating + replay + pinning;
  PUB verify-stamp-cache-fanout; DELIVER re-fan and re-pin. Test: N
  subscribers, one root, 100\% delivery, total order by stamp.
\item \textbf{The root-claim state machine} (\S\ref{sec:roots}): the
  transitions and the defer gate with its three evidence tiers, as one
  module with one transition function; then the evidence planes
  (beacons with verify-don't-trust, self-verification, death sweeps).
  Test: the decision table directly --- farther beacons never defer;
  ghosts never defer under strict mode; every flip logs once.
\item \textbf{The repair plane} (\S\ref{sec:timing}): one tick driving
  renewals (adaptive), pending retries, replication, eviction, expiry,
  beacon/verify cadence; the constants table verbatim; complete
  teardown on stop.
\item \textbf{The convergence plane} (\S\ref{sec:pubsub},
  \S\ref{sec:lifecycle}): one sync operation, the policy table
  verbatim --- delta-gated cohort replication with keepalives,
  replay-up (hw) and split-union (lw) with the one-shot guard, the
  empty-root probe, kills with provisional authorship, and leave with
  evidence-drain, \emph{acknowledged} handoff, and silence.
\item \textbf{Prove the invariants} (\S\ref{sec:invariants}): write all
  eighteen as executable tests before tuning anything --- including the
  ones marked UNFENCED, which are where this implementation has been
  wrong most often. Then characterize
  on a live mesh --- healed delivery after a renewal cycle is the
  headline number, single runs are noise (REPS $\ge$ 5), and no verdict
  comes from a seconds-old client.
\end{enumerate}

Four traps this document exists to prevent. \emph{Forgetting the
waypoint-pop rule}: you will wrongly predict deadlocks and ``fix'' them
into churn storms. \emph{Adding a root-claim site instead of a guard in
the one decision table}: you will recreate the patch-interaction bug
class the 4.20 refactor killed. \emph{Adding a data-movement mechanism
instead of a sync-policy row}: every mechanism you add carries its own
trigger, quench, and guard, and interacts with all the others --- the
4.22--4.24 incident chain, including two backbone collapses, was
exactly this trap sprung four times. \emph{Letting a departing or
transient node plant standing state}: replicate-from-a-leaver reads as
a cheap durability win and is a cascade seed; the principal-liveness
rule exists because the cheap win collapsed a fleet.

\subsection{What you will still have to derive}

``From this note alone'' is a claim about the \emph{model} and the
\emph{wire contract}, and it should be read narrowly. Four things are
not here, and a reimplementer will meet all four.

\emph{The constants are ours, not derived.} Every value in the timing
table (\S\ref{sec:timing}) carries its reason, which is what lets you
re-tune it --- but the numbers came from a mesh of tens to low hundreds
of nodes on consumer hardware and cloud droplets. At a different scale
or a different latency profile they are a starting point, not a
specification.

\emph{The failure catalogue is partial by construction.} The robustness
section (\S\ref{sec:robustness}) describes failures that have happened
to this implementation. Everything in the open issue register is a
failure we know about and have not fixed; what neither list contains is
the class nobody has hit yet, and there is no reason to think the
network has finished producing them.

\emph{Nine invariants are marked UNFENCED} (\S\ref{sec:invariants}). Those are
statements of intent that the code is believed to satisfy. A second
implementation that satisfies the fenced ones and violates an unfenced
one will not be caught by anything either project runs today.

\emph{Interoperability is unproven.} No second implementation exists,
so every ``cross-implementation'' test above is a test nobody has run.
The first one will find things --- most likely in canonicalization,
where byte-equality is required and the specification is a paragraph.
That is not a caveat on the document so much as the reason to build the
second implementation.

% APPENDIX: THE LIFE OF A MESSAGE
% =====================================================================
\section{Appendix: The Life of a Message}

Publisher $P$ (browser, us-east), subscribers $S_{1..12}$, fresh topic
$T=\{$region:\,useast, owner:\,null, name:\,'lobby', write:\,'open'$\}$.

\begin{enumerate}
\item $S_1$ subscribes: SUB routes toward $T$, terminates at $R$ (the
  closest live peer) $\rightarrow$ \texttt{become('sub-terminal')} ---
  $R$ is now the root, beacons immediately, self-verifies at 6\,s.
  $S_1$ is seated, pinned by the deliver-from, renews via $R$.
\item $S_{2..12}$ subscribe; all reach $R$ (beacons steer near-misses).
  At 21 directs, $R$ promotes $S_2$ to a child relay with a batch of 8
  --- the tree widens.
\item $P$ publishes: envelope signed with $P$'s author key; PUB routes
  toward $T$ (hint-assisted); $R$ verifies, stamps
  (\texttt{publishTs}, \texttt{seq=1}), caches, fans to directs and the
  child (which re-fans), replicates eagerly to its 2-member cohort.
  $P$, being subscribed, receives its own message --- observation
  confirms; retries stop.
\item $P$ kills the message: signed kill routes to $R$; authorship
  matches; tombstone recorded (\texttt{seq=2}), del marker fans down,
  cohort updated eagerly. A later \texttt{since:'all'} joiner replays
  survivors only.
\item $R$ leaves: drain (nothing pending), handoff of $T$'s
  cache+tombstones to heir $H$ (\texttt{from:\,R}); $H$ acknowledges
  (\texttt{handoffack}) inside the first round, so no retry and no
  fallback fire; notify, silence. $H$ purges $R$'s beacon, keeps the
  claim, beacons; renewals re-seat everyone under $H$ within one fast
  cycle. Total order continues above the inherited \texttt{lastTs}.
\item Late that night a cold reader $C$ subscribes from a fresh node
  that happens to sit closer to $T$ than $H$: $C$'s SUB terminates at
  itself and it becomes root --- empty. The birth probe pulls $T$'s
  history from the cohort and from $H$ (on $C$'s lookup path) within a
  second; $C$ serves the full timeline. $H$ re-homes under $C$ as a
  child; its \texttt{lw} advertisement confirms nothing older is
  stranded. No mechanism special-cased $C$'s newness --- the same
  policies that run everywhere ran here (I-7).
\end{enumerate}
